Sim-to-real balance control of an 8-DoF quadruped using ROS 2 and embedded complementary filtering

Romil Chetankumar Modi

2025

Full text (PDF)

Low-cost legged robot research is constrained by a reproducibility gap: open-source platforms at or below £300 either lack onboard closed-loop balance, omit independent sensor validation, or both. This paper addresses that gap with the Rombotics Q8, a self-balancing quadruped actuated by eight high-torque servo motors (two revolute joints per leg: femur and tibia), driven by an ESP32 dual-core microcontroller and a PCA9685 16-channel PWM driver. Orientation is estimated at 100 Hz using an MPU6050 six-axis IMU fused through a complementary filter (α = 0.98). Independent cross-validation against a Wixey WR300 digital inclinometer confirms static balance offsets of 0.28 ± 0.12° (pitch) and −0.33 ± 0.08° (roll), with mean absolute errors of 0.18° (pitch) and 0.21° (roll) over 20 static trials; yaw was not cross-validated as the inclinometer measures tilt angle only — an external validation step absent from all reviewed open-source platforms at this cost tier. A per-axis PID controller with first-order output smoothing compensates pitch, roll, and yaw disturbances: pitch and roll recover from ±10° step inputs in 0.41 ± 0.05 s, yaw in 0.61 ± 0.07 s. A PWM-space piecewise-linear tibia-coupling law couples each tibia servo inversely to its femur partner, ensuring approximate four-point ground contact during balance corrections without an additional joint-angle sensor; the approximation error is analytically bounded to <7 mm over the ±10° balance operating range. The 10 ms control loop executes with 0.23 ms jitter (max observed 10.47 ms) measured over 10 000 consecutive iterations on physical hardware. Long-duration testing (n = 5 runs; preliminary characterisation) indicates near-linear gyroscope drift of 0.003° min−1 (pitch/roll) and 0.0033° min−1 (yaw) over 60 min. Preliminary open-loop trot trials on a single flat surface establish a directed-locomotion capability baseline at 0.18 m s−1, providing a foundation for future closed-loop gait development. The complete platform costs under £250 in components and is fully open-source, providing a reproducible low-cost baseline for legged-robot research.
